Navier-Weber Fluids: Darcy-Weber Pressure Drops and Capacities

Our goal is to introduce the Darcy-Weber pressure drop formulas and capacity tables based on the Navier-Weber fluid equations. These pressure drop and capacity formulas distinguish Navier-Weber from Navier-Stokes models of steady-state fluid flow, especially for ultracapillary and high velocity flows. This means reviewing Hagen-Poiseuille’s analytical solution for Navier-Stokes. We generate new Darcy-Weber capacity tables which increasingly diverge from Navier-Stokes in certain physical regimes depending on Reynolds and Weber’s numbers Re and We.

In this post we discuss pressure drops and capacity tables according to the Navier-Weber fluid equations. This provides experiments to distinguish Navier-Weber from the Navier-Stokes model, according to the accuracy of our pressure drop formulas given below.

The standard method of computing pressure drops is through Darcy-Weisbach formula \[\Delta p = f_{DW} \frac{L}{D} \frac{\rho U^2}{2},\] where \(f_{DW}\) is the Darcy-Weisbach factor.

The factor \(f_{DW}\) is not a simple constant, but is a dynamic factor depending on the physical parameters of the fluid flow. These physical parameters are defined by the dimensionless Navier-Stokes equation, e.g. Reynolds numbers \[\text{Re}:=\frac{\rho U D}{\mu}.\]

Our study of Navier-Weber implies the introduction of a new physical parameter we call Weber’s number \[\text{We}:= \frac{16 \nu_{non} U^2}{\rho D^4}. \]

The pressure drop formulas \(\Delta p\) also generate relations between volumetric capacity \(Q\) and the fluid parameters \(\Delta p\), \(\nu_{non}\), \(\rho\), diameter \(D\), length \(L\), \(f_{DW}\), etc.. These capacity relations will be described below. We include python scripts below which generate the Darcy-Weber capacities as a function of the physical parameters.

Assumptions: Incompressible Steady-State Laminar Cylindrical Flow

Throughout this post we consider fluids flowing at steady-state in a long transmission pipeline of diameter \(D\) and length \(L\). Our derivations assume the fluid is modelled in cylindrical coordinates \((x,y,z) \mapsto (r, \theta, z)\), and satisfying the following assumptions:

  • that viscosity and density are constant ;
  • that the flow has attained steady state \(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}=0\);
  • that the flow is developed \(\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}=0\);
  • that the flow is axisymmetric and parallel \(v_r=0\), \(v_\theta=0\), and \(v_z=v_z(r)\) depends only on \(r\).

Darcy-Weisbach Pressure Drop and Capacity Formulas

The usual capacity tables are based on the Darcy-Weisbach formulas for pressure drops in a cylindrical pipeline of diameter \(D\) and length \(L\). Let \(U\) be the fluid velocity, and let \(p\) be the fluid pressure.

A basic force balance equation requires the existence of a wall strain \(\tau_W\) satisfying \[ (\frac{\pi D^2}{4})\Delta p = (\pi D L) \tau_W.\] This force balance equation implies \[\Delta p = 4 \frac{L}{D} \tau.\]

The Navier-Stokes definition of wall strain is based on Newtonian viscosity \(\tau(r)=-\frac{du}{dr}|_{r=R}\). The standard approach normalizes \(\tau\) against the bulk kinetic energy \(\rho U^2/2\) of the fluid, and the Darcy-Weisbach factor \(f_{DW}\) is defined by the expression \[\tau:=\frac{1}{4}f_{DW}\frac{\rho U^2}{2}.\] Here \(f_{DW}\) is a factor depending on the material properties of the fluid. This implies the Darcy-Weisbach pressure drop formula \[\Delta p = f_{DW} \frac{L}{D} \frac{\rho U^2}{2}.\]

Darcy-Weisbach factor \(f_{DW}\) and Normalization

The Darcy-Weisbach formula is a general assumption for fluids of all regimes, turbulent or laminar. The Darcy-Weisbach factor \(f_{DW}\) is a dimensionless factor. There is interesting dimensional argument for proving that \(f_{DW}\) depends on two physical parameters, namely \(Re\) and \(\epsilon/D\) “wall roughness”. This is derived from the Navier-Stokes equation, by normalizing all the variables into dimensionless numbers. When we rewrite Navier-Stokes in dimensionless form, we see that \(\frac{1}{\text{Re}}\) is the only parameter in the dimensionless equation.

Similarly we will find the Navier-Weber fluid equations and Darcy-Weber factor depends essentially on two physical factors, namely \(1/\text{Re}\) Reynolds numbers and the Weber parameter \[\text{We}:= \frac{16 \nu_{non} U^2}{\rho D^4}.\] In dimensionless coordinates, the Darcy-Weber factor \(f_{DW}\) is a function of \(\text{Re}, W\) and satisfies \[ f_{DW}(\text{Re}, W) = \frac{16}{\text{Re}} {\dot{\gamma}}^* + 4\alpha_{non} \frac{W}{\text{Re}} {{\dot{\gamma}}^*}^3.\]

Darcy-Weisbach Capacity Tables

Let \(Q=U (\pi D^2/4)\) be the volumetric flow capacity. Substituting \(Q\) into the above equations yields the Darcy-Weisbach pressure drop formula: \[ \Delta p = \frac{8}{\pi^2} f_{\text{DW}} \rho \frac{L}{D^5} Q^2. \]

The pressure drop predicted by Darcy-Weisbach is assumed to be caused by the shear stresses and “friction” of the fluid along the surface of the pipe. The formula depends on the No-Slip boundary condition.

The Darcy-Weisbach pressure drop formula gives the standard formula predicting the volumetric capacity \(Q\) satisfying a given pressure drop \(\Delta p\). Rearranging for \(Q\) yields:

\[Q = \left( \frac{\pi^2 \Delta p}{8 \rho f_{DW}} \right)^{1/2} D^{5/2} L^{-1/2}\]

This capacity formula directly generates the standard capacity tables in the codebooks [ref].

Steady-State Laminar Darcy-Weber Pressure Drop and Capacity Formulas

Our goal is to modify the Darcy-Weisbach formula to include Weberian effects. In the standard Navier-Stokes formulation, the viscosity is defined by the relation \(\tau(r)=-\mu \frac{du}{dr}\). For the Navier-Weber fluid model, we adapt \(\tau\) to include Weber’s nonlinear shear-thickening term:

\[\tau_w(r):=-\mu \frac{du}{dr} - \alpha_{non} \nu_{non} (\frac{du}{dr})^3.\] We assume \(\mu>0\) and \(\nu_{non}>0\).

If we define \({\dot{\gamma}}(r) := -\frac{du}{dr}\) as the strain rate, then the shear rate \(\dot{\gamma}\) satisfies a cubic polynonial equation:

\[ \dot{\gamma}^3 + (\frac{\mu}{\alpha_{non} \nu_{non}} \dot{\gamma} - \frac{\Delta p r}{2 \alpha_{non}\nu_{non} L}=0.\]

We can actually solve for \(\dot{\gamma}\) explicitly using Cardano’s closed-form solution. Ultimately we find the following Darcy-Weber pressure drop formula in steady-state laminar flows:

\[\Delta p = 32 \mu \frac{L}{D^2} U + 4 \alpha_{non} \nu_{non} \frac{L}{D^4} U^3.\]

The above formula immediately implies the existence of two pressure drop regimes:

  • When \(r\to 0^+\), the linear Newtonian viscosity dominates \(\mu \dot{\gamma} \propto r\). Near the pipe centerline, the Newtonian terms dominate and we obtain the parabolic velocity scaling \(u(r) \approx u(0)-\frac{\Delta p}{4 \mu L}r^2\).
  • When \(r\to R^-\), the nonlinear Weber shear-thickening term dominates near the boundary wall $ {non} {non} {}^3$, and the wall shear is blunted like \(r^{4/3}\).

Recall the volumetric capacity \(Q\) is defined by \(Q=\frac{4U}{\pi D^2}\). Substituting into the above pressure drop formula we obtain:

\[\Delta p = \frac{128}{\pi} \mu \frac{L}{D^4}Q + \frac{256}{\pi^3} \alpha_{non} \nu_{non} \frac{L}{D^{10}} Q^3.\]

Now we want to solve the above cubic equation for \(Q\) as a function of \(\Delta p\). We can directly solve for \(Q\) using Cardano’s solution to the cubic. There is another interesting solution obtained by hyperbolic sine functions. Ultimately we find:

\[Q=Q(\Delta p)=\pi D^3 \sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{3 \alpha_{non} \nu_{non}}} \sinh [\frac{1}{3} \text{arcsinh}(\frac{3\sqrt{6}D \Delta p}{256 L}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{non} \nu_{non}}{\mu^3}})] .\]

Let \(\Omega\) be defined as be the dimensionless Weber hydraulic parameter \[\Omega:=\frac{\Delta p_{\text{Weber}}}{\Delta p_{\text{Poiseuille}}} =\frac{\text{We}}{1/\text{Re}} \cdot \left( \frac{\alpha_{\text{non}} \dot{\gamma}_w^2}{2} \right).\]

Recall the Taylor expansions \(\text{arcsinh}(x) \approx x\) and \(\sinh(x) \approx x\) for small \(x \approx 0\).

In the low pressure limit when \(\Omega <<1\) the capacity formula becomes approximately \[Q \approx \frac{\pi}{128} \frac{1}{\mu} \Delta p D^4.\] This recovers the classical Hagen-Poiseuille capacity formula.

In the high pressure limit where \(\Omega >>1\), then we approximate \(Q\) using the asymptotic approximations of hyperbolic sine function, namely \[\sinh(\frac{1}{3} \text{arcsinh}(\Omega)) \approx \frac{1}{2} (2\Omega)^{1/3}.\]

This implies the approximation \[ Q \approx \pi (\frac{1}{256 \alpha_{non} \nu_{non}})^{1/3} D^{10/3} (\frac{\Delta p }{L})^{1/3}.\] Therefore in the limit \(\Omega >>1\) we find linear visocity \(\mu\) cancels, and we recover Weber’s non-linear capacity formula.
