What is the Universal Goal of Machine Learning?

We propose the universal goal of machine learning ought to be learning the most significant coarse bits of a correlation.

A review of various algorithms suggests that every machine learning problem centres around predicting whether points belong or don’t belong to the support of a target measure, i.e. to predict whether inputs and output are correlated. This is the measure theory extension of deciding whether an edge \(E\) exists between two vertices \(x_1, x_2\) in a large graph, c.f. (Tao 2005). Indeed we argue here that deciding supports of correlations is the universal decision problem in mathematics.

Chollett (Chollet 2021) proposes four different types of machine learning algorithms, namely supervised, unsupervised, self-supervised, and reinforcement learning. Supervised learning typically looks to “label” data based on training batches and gradient flows. The gradient flows towards weights which minimize the error loss, a differentiable function of the weights. Thus supervised learning uses labels, weights, and loss functions to generate measures approximating the target correlation. This raises the simple question: Is the goal of training to optimize the loss function on training batches? The gradient method has a limited application in practice, because eventually the numerical distances between \(y_{pred}\) and \(y_{target}\) – as measured by the error loss function – does not statistically reflect the “close-ness” or “far-ness” between prediction and target. Eventually the numerical distances are not significant, and targets are equally “missed by a hair, or missed by a mile”. Here the error loss becomes insignificant, and we claim this is the inflection point between under- and over-fitting in supervised learning models. This inflection point is basically the dimension of \(Z'\) and related to the “Renyi information dimension” (Rényi 1959) of \(Z'\) and the target correlation.

Tao-Szemeredi Approximations and Most Significant Bits

When predicting uncertain events \(X\) or correlations \(\pi\), we find there are certain coarse bits which are generally the most significant, and such that \(X, \pi\) are independant of conditioning on any further bits. These nearly optimal approximations are the focus of Tao-Szemeredi Regularity Lemma (Tao 2005). The most significant bits are defined by an optimization program defined below. In fact Tao-Szemeredi provides two approximations, the coarse and fine approximations \(Z, Z'\). Both \(Z,Z'\) have bounded entropy, independant of \(H(X)\). Both \(Z,Z'\) are coarse approximations, but \(Z'\) is finer having a certain optimality property with respect to deterministic reductions \(X \mapsto W\) satisfying \(W\mapsto Z'\).

The Tao-Szemeredi program looks to construct the best coarse approximation to predicting a given correlation, and suggests a measure theoretic analogue to diophantine approximation, i.e. find the best approximation with the simplest coefficients.

These coarse approximations are the “critical insights” basic to advertising, e.g. Critical Mass.

The basic premise of machine learning is that training data contains information statistically significant to predicting the target event, c.f. (Chollet 2021). But not all bits are significant to a given problem. Here enters Tao-Szemeredi, which looks for coarse sub-bits of the variables which are maximally correlated to the target. Tao-Szemeredi implies that correlations decompose into a deterministic part and a random statistically independant part. The Tao-Szemeredi algorithm produces two approximations, the fine and coarse approximations \(Z', Z\). The fine approximation \(Z'\) is nearly optimal such that \(\pi\) is independant of conditioning on any further sub-bits.

Tao-Szemeredi Algorithm

Let \(\delta > 0\) be choice of small real number. Let \(F:{\mathbf{R}}_+ \to {\mathbf{R}}_+\) be a non-decreasing function. Initialize with \(Z=(Z_1, Z_2)=(0,0)\). These are choices.

Let \(Z'\) be a minimizer of \[\text{min}_{Z'}[H(X|Z')+\frac{H(Z')}{F(H(Z))}]\] where the minimum ranges over all deterministic reductions \(Z'\) satisfying \(X\mapsto Z' \mapsto Z\).

If \(H(X|Z)-H(X|Z') \geq \delta\), then replace \(Z:=Z'\) and restart the minimization program. Otherwise break, and return \(Z, Z'\) the coarse and fine approximations, respectively.

The goal of the minimization program is to simultaneously minimize the conditional entropy \(H(X|Z')\) and the relative entropy \(H(Z')/H(F(Z))\). The fine approximation \(Z'\) has the further property of being a nearly optimal approximation of \(X\) in the sense that \[I(X:W|Z') = H(X|Z')-H(X|Z', W) \leq H(W)/F(H(Z))\] for any random variable \(W\) satisfying \(X\mapsto W \mapsto Z'\). Therefore \(X\) is approximately independant of any further bits conditioned on \(Z'\). We consider this a key property of the fine approximation.

We claim that Tao-Szemeredi is the perfect balance of “optimization” and “generalization”. Thus we argue that the construction of Tao-Szemeredi “best approximations” \(Z, Z'\) ought to be the universal goal of machine learning.
