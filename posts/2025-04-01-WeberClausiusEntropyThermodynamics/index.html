Weber, Clausius, Entropy, and Thermodynamics

The classical reference for entropy is Clausius 1865 (Clausius 1865), which introduces the term \(S\) as “the entropy of a body, from the Greek word ‘τροπή’ (transformation), because in the processes of nature this quantity always changes in a definite direction.”

Clausius further states “The energy of the universe is constant. The entropy of the universe tends to a maximum.” This contains the principle of the First Law of Thermodynamics, and Clausius’ Second Law of Thermodynamics. .

Deriving First Law of Thermodynamics from Mechanics and Conservation of Energy

In Gibbs’ book (Gibbs 1902) we find the problem of deriving the First Law of Thermodynamics from mechanics, namely \[ dU = TdS - \sum F dx\] where \(W=\sum Fdx\) represents work on the mechanical system, and \(dU\) represents the change of internal potential energy of the system. Many references describe the First Law as represented by \(dU = T dS - PdV\) where \(PdV\) represents pressure across the change in volume. This latter term is equivalent to the work performed by the pressure along the boundary of the volume. Note pressure is defined only along the boundary of the volume in the above expression.

We derive the First Law of Thermodynamics from Weberian mechanics as follows.

First recall the primary equation of classical mechanics, namely Newton’s \(F=F_{local}=ma\), which we rewrite as \(F_{local}-ma=0\). This latter step is simple and important. It suggests replacing \(F_{local}\) the sum of all forces acting on \(x\) with the sum of all forces acting on \(x\) in the universe \(F=F_{local}+F_{nonlocal}\). Thus we replace \(F_{local}-ma=0\) with the equation of dynamic equilibrium \[\sum F=0.\]

This balance of forces becomes the primary law of mechanics. It includes the equations of Newtonian mechanics by explaining the mass acceleration term \(-ma\) as equal to the nonlocal force \(F\) exerted by the universe at infinity on \(x\). We experience inertial masses as constant because the universe at infinity is basically constant on average in our reference frame. The sky at infinity does not move that much relative to our frame. This is a form of Mach’s principle that inertial mass is defined by our motion relative to the stars at infinity (Mach 1883). See Halton Arp’s lecture on intrinsic red shift.

The Principle of Dynamic Equilibrium can equivalently be expressed as the Principle of Zero Energy \(\sum U = 0\). We emphasize that this expression represents the sum of all local and nonlocal interaction energies. This means energy is conserved globally at all times, but locally varies according to \(\Delta U_{local} = -\Delta U_{nonlocal}\). Stability of nonlocal interaction potential yields the usual Conservation of Energy of closed systems. Therefore energy can be created and destroyed locally, but the sum over all interactions is zero. “The only constant is everything adds up to zero.”

Entropy is Random Kinetic Energy, Work is Parallel Coherent Kinetic Energy.

The Weberian perspective on thermodynamics is that energy strictly manifests in two forms: potential \(U\) and kinetic \(T\). Weber defines potential as the potential for productive work, i.e. the potential for matter to be put in linear parallel motion having kinetic energy. Here “work” means applying a force to an object in a linear motion, i.e. apply a force through a distance. Thus work consists of matter being put into relative motion.

The distinction belonging to the First Law of Thermodynamics is that matter in motion, can be decomposed into “Random” plus “NonRandom” motion. The Random motion is incoherent and does not add to any large scale bulk linear motion. The parallel part of the kinetic energy is that part of the motion which results in productive work. The random incoherent part is the entropy expression represented by the term “\(T.dS\)” as we propose below.

The internal potential energy of a system is extracted by the environment into work, but this work is not necessarily coherent nor productive. That part of the work which is unproductive is represented by the entropy term and repesents random KE. This represents the Clausius kinetic view on entropy and temperature.

The thermodynamic definition of Temperature is according to the formula \[T=\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\] of the two extensive variables \(U, S\) of potential energy and entropy, respectively. There is interesting remark in [BBC, In Our Time, Science, Episode on Heat] which gives the Scottish Glaswegian definition of entropy as representing the loss of available work, or in our terms, the loss of available productive work in a system. Thus entropy represents that part of the kinetic energy which is random and adds up incoherently to zero, and represents that part of kinetic energy which does not contribute to the productive work performed by the system.

The thermodynamic variables \(U,S\) are extensive with respect to a system \(X\) in the sense that they are \(1\)-homogeneous with respect to the state variables. The ratio \(T = dU/dS\) implies \(T\) is an intensive variable (\(0\)-homogeneous with respect to state variables) designating “temperature” (and not kinetic energy per the usual notation). This amounts to the Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics. But it still begs the question “how to represent the random kinetic energy?”

Remark. The 19th and 20th century had a broader heterogeneous view of energy and energy radiation, e.g. photon radiation, gravitational radiation, electromagnetic, etc.. The Weber perspective, by contrast, follows Ampere’s original idea that primary laws of physics need be homogeneous. Thus Weber explains all forms of energy transfer as various expressions of a homogeneous system of energy transfer, namely between potential and kinetic energy according to Weber electrodynamics.

Principle of Minimal Internal Potential Energy

Water flows.

Water flows wherever it has the opportunity to flow.

Water will flow whenever it can flow.

Water never forfeits the opportunity to flow.

This is like electrical discharges. All the potential at high voltage exists, but it does not discharge without a conducting path, without an opportunity. Moreover it immediately discharges if there is a conducting path.

This surprises us sometimes. It accounts for the particular direction of time in our opinion. We remind the reader that we take the Machian perspective of time as “matter in motion” (Mach 1883).

Where did we first learn of this idea? We can’t recall…

The principle of minimal internal potential energy expresses the idea that any system \(X\) in an environment \(E\) will have all its available capacity for work extracted by the environment. We are reminded of a scene at UofT, an old Soviet era professor saying, “Well, if there was any object [X], then it’s gone they stole it.” Once we know the environment has a positive need and demand for work, then it’s clear that any available potential energy will be extracted by the environment. This is why the hot cup of coffee becomes cold. It’s subject to the environment. We are always competing with the environment, which looks to seize upon all available potential and translate this energy into incoherent kinetic energies which have no bulk state of motion. If one leaves potential energy subject to the environment, it is almost certainly gone when we come back.

To belabour the point yet further, consider the basic so-called law of supply and demand. The law includes the proposition that wherever there is supply, and wherever there is demand, there is also the transfer of the goods. This represents our basic idea about the Principle of Minimal Potential Energy. It is a property of the external environment. The internal potential energy is only minimized to the extent that the environment can absorb this potential and translate it into kinetic energy (either random or coherent).

Basic thermodynamics states that entropy \(S\) is defined as the Fenchel-Legendre transform \(S=U^*\) of the potential energy. Therefore the entropy \(S\) is maximized wherever the potential energy is minimized according to the constraints. Compare (Cullen 1969).

A similar idea is that minimum potential energy is equivalent to maximal environmental extraction of work. If a system has the potential to do productive work, and the environment has a need for productive energy, then the environment will extract the energy and convert into KE. This is the idea that water flows and will flow. Therefore states with some initial potential energy will have this potential converted into kinetic energy at the first environmental opportunity. A state always reduces to a ground state, relative to the environment, after the environment has extracted the maximal amount of productive work from the system’s potential.

Clausius Statement: “Heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body without external work being done.”

How is this interpreted by Weber? We have two bodies \(B_1\) and \(B_2\), say \(B_2\) is the environment. Suppose that the random KE of \(B_1\) is less than the random KE of \(B_2\). Then Clausius says there is a definite transfer of KE from \(B_2\) to \(B_1\) in the absence of any external work. Contrapositively, there is no transfer of heat (random KE) from \(B_1\) to \(B_2\) without external work.

Remark on Shannon Entropy and Thermodynamic Entropy

The Shannon information entropy is basically \(\log(\text{number of states})\). If a physical system has high entropy, then there is alot of random KE and the positions and velocities of any particles cannot be known with any certainty. On average the magnitude of the velocities might be known, but the direction and position of these particles will not be known. The information about the position and velocities has alot of random variation, as determined by the random KE, and this corresponds to information uncertainty.

Conclusion

We end with some questions. This post is contains our initial notes on Weber-Clausius entropy. Some concluding questions:

  1. Where is the Second Law of Thermodynamics in our discussion? The basic reference is Clausius’. Standard reference is that Second Law states \(T dS \geq \delta Q\) in an isolated system. I.e. \(dU = T dS \geq \delta Q\) for every isolated system. This implies that entropy is always increasing in any isolated system. (Clausius 1865) What is the Weber mechanics explanation?

  2. The Clausius entropy says that work cannot be performed by a machine which does not have a cold sink. How does Weber mechanics interpret this idea? The Weber viewpoint implies that entropy always involves heat and thermal energy, which ex definitio, is random kinetic energy. This amounts to the increase in average kinetic energy in the environment.

  3. What is random kinetic energy? We interpret this random KE that part of kinetic energy which does not contribute to the parallel linear kinetic energy of the system. By definition it is nonparallel (random incoherent).
