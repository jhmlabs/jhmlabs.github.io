On Light and Sansbury’s Third Alternative

Light (or “phos” φοσ) is the fundamental phenomenon of nature. Discovering properties and qualities of light is the key question underlying almost all physics, both classic and modern. The most outstanding question remains whether light is something like a particle or something like a wave. The particle/corpuscle/photon model originates with Newton’s investigations into Opticks (Newton 1704), while the wave/undulatory model was proposed by Young and Huygens and supported by Young’s classic double-slit experiment (Young 1801). In classical physics, the diverse “particle” and “wave” models of light was cause of significant tension. Eventually 20th century physics formalized this tension in Bohr’s philosophy of “complementarity”, c.f. the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum physics. We have critiqued Bohr’s complementary in our paper on the foundations of special relativity. Indeed we argue that Einstein’s SR is based on the erroneous hypothesis that “light travels in vacuum” at a speed which is independant of every inertial frame. This property simultaneously contradicts both the particle and wave models of light.

In this article we discuss a third classical alternative to the particle-wave models of light. This third alternative originates in the work of Ralph N Sansbury (Sansbury 2012). Light, as we argue following Sansbury, has a different explanation. Sansbury proposes that “light is not something that travels at all, but is rather the effect of cumulative oscillations of charge in atomic nuclei.” In this explanation, the speed of light is reinterpreted as a time delay, namely the time required for subatomic oscillations to accumulate sufficient energy and excite orbital electrons which become detectable as light in target receivers. Sansbury writes “Light itself need not be mysteriously produced by instantaneous transitions between energy levels and then propagated as a wave or photon or a probabilistic photon with a velocity equal to the speed of light. Instead, light or radiation in general, may be regarded as the effect of oscillations of charged particles in a source that produce, instantaneously at a distance, in phase oscillations of charged particles in the primary receiver, first inside atomic nuclei, then after a delay, oscillations of electrons, e.g., of free electrons or of bound electrons where the widening of orbits of bound atomic electrons leads to their ejection.”

Sansbury’s original approach involved Coulomb electrostatic interactions as the force law between electric charges in motion. However we emphasize Wilhelm Edouard Weber’s electrodynamic force law. Weber’s potential implies all forces act instantaneously and at-a-distance. Sansbury’s third alternative is based on cumulative effects of instantaneous oscillations of charge. Sansbury assumes “that light or radio signals are the result of instantaneous forces at the same repeated frequency acting on atomic nuclei in the eye or in a radio antenna, [which] after a delay, increase in amplitude to produce a detectable effect [in the receiver].” Again we repeat that light signals do not have a speed, but rather subatomic nuclei have a time delay before accumulating enough energy to be distinguishable from background noise.

We motivate Sansbury’s idea with an analogy. Suppose you have a friend visiting from Toronto to Ottawa. To receive your Toronto friend at your home in Ottawa, the door of your house only needs to be open during their expected time of arrival. The door of your house can be closed during your friend’s expected travel time, and this will not interfere with their arrival at your house. Your travelling friend only needs the door open and unobstructed at their expected time of arrival to enter. That’s because your friend is something that travels. However light, argues Sansbury, is not something that travels. To receive light your doors must be open during most of the travel time. Light does not travel, but attenuates. The door can actually be closed during the expected time of arrival and the light signal is still received!

The burden of Sansbury’s incredible third alternative that light does not travel, is to provide explanations of all the keystone physics experiment involving light. E.g.

Sansbury’s Classical Explanation of Young’s Double Slit Experiment

Our goal is to describe the Weber-Sansbury classical interpretation of Young’s surprising experiment.

Historically Young’s demonstration of dark and bright fringes in the double slit experiment provided key evidence for Huygens-Fresnel’s wave theory of light in opposition to Newton’s corpuscular “photon” theory. Sansbury briefly accounts for the observed interference fringes in his book (Sansbury 2012) as follows:

” [T]he double slit diffraction pattern of alternating dark and light bands on the receiver screen due to a light source partially obstructed by an opaque screen with two slits in it can be explained as follows: light from the source is scattered by the atoms in the slit edges which together with the source act on the atoms and molecules in the receiver screen. Because there are different \(r/c\) delays between the slit edge secondary sources and different points on the screen, in phase oscillations are produced at some points and out of phase cancelling oscillations are produced at other points. There is no need for probabilistic photons or the interpretation that the observed alternating dark and light bands are a probability distribution.”

“The different delays from secondary oscillations in charges in the edges of the slits to the dark bands […] produce out-of-phase delays, and, to the bands of light, in-phase delays.”

Slits and Oscillating Dipoles

Here is the basic idea. Suppose we are studying Young’s double slit experiment, with a coherent light source. According to Sansbury’s hypothesis, light is not generated by electromagnetic waves nor by photons, but rather the cumulative effect of instantaneous electrodynamic action at a distance.

Claim: the primary light source is generated by an electric dipole oscillating at a fixed frequency.

Claim: The source dipole induces secondary oscillating dipoles in the electron clouds of each slit. If the source dipole is oscillating at fixed frequency, then the induced dipoles in the slits will oscillate at the same frequency (after a delay).

Claim: The receiver screen interacts with the primary dipole and the secondary dipoles at every slit.

Actually we argue that the secondary dipoles at the slit are the dominant force acting on the receiver. The force depends on the relative orientations of the primary dipole and the slits, i.e. whether the primary dipole is parallel or transverse to the slits.

Claim: The net effect of the oscillating secondary dipoles acting on the receiver screen causes the fringe interference pattern.

This is the essential idea behind Ralph Sansbury’s classical interpretation of the Young’s double slit experiment.
