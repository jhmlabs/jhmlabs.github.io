Physical Experiments to distinguish Navier-Weber from Navier-Stokes

We continue to develop the Navier-Weber fluid equations as direct competitor to the Navier-Stokes model. Our goal is to ultimately establish:

  1. that Navier-Weber is a more accurate model of fluid dynamics at high velocities;
  2. that fluids are conservative, not dissipative;
  3. that smooth initial datum has unique smooth global Navier-Weber solutions.

We maintain that fluids are conservative, not dissipative. We argue that fluids are fundamentally electrodynamic, although neutral in every bulk volume. We argue that Keesom’s proof that dipoles thermally average to a repulsive force establishes the observed molecular attraction between bulk electroneutral molecules.

The one-dimensional Navier-Weber equations are defined by the Conservation of Energy \[\delta K_{rel} = \delta W = -\delta U.\]

The virtual work integral \(\delta W\) is computed equal to \[\delta W = \iiint (\mathbb{T}+\mathbb{V})\delta(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x})dx=0\] where \(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{V}\) are the nonlinear and viscoelastic terms \[\mathbb{T} = \nu_{non}(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x})^3\] and \[\mathbb{V}=\eta_{v} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}).\]

Integration by parts and \(\delta (\frac{\partial u}{\partial x})=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\delta u)\) implies \[\iiint_\Omega(\mathbb{T}+\mathbb{V}) \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\delta u) dx = ()|_\Omega - \iiint div(\mathbb{T}+\mathbb{V}) \delta u.dx\]

The \(1D\) Navier-Weber equations are therefore defined as:

\[\rho(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}) = \frac{\partial p}{\partial x} + 3\nu_{non}(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x})^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \eta_v \frac{\partial }{\partial t}(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}). \]

The LHS of the Navier-Weber equations is effective mass times acceleration, as represented by \(-\frac{\partial K}{\partial t}\) where \(K\) is the relational kinetic energy. The general \(3D\) Navier-Weber equations are obtained similarly.

Physical Tests of Navier-Weber versus Navier-Stokes

The purpose of this post is to present physical experiments which distinguish between Navier-Weber and Navier-Stokes fluid models. The results are quite interesting.

Classical Navier-Stokes assumes viscosity is linear Newtonian.

The viscosity terms in Navier-Weber are the divergences of \(\mathbb{T} + \mathbb{V}\). These divergences are nonlinear and non Newtonian. These viscosity divergences have immediate physical interpretations.

Shear-Thickening

The first consequence of Navier-Weber is that effective viscosity increases quadratically with strain rate \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\). Indeed we find \(div(\mathbb{T})=3\nu_{\text{non}}(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x})^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}\)

Example: Consider pipeline transmission of hydrogen blended natural gases. These blends require high volumetric flow velocities to maintain constant energy throughput. Consequently near pipe walls, the molecules will experience large velocity gradients and will experience Weber shear thickening. This means the fluid experiences higher wall drag and pressure drops at high flow rates. Therefore the standard Colebrook-White and Darcy-Weisbach values do not apply. New Navier-Weber calculations will be required.

Example: Another consequence is that microleaks will be slow and constrained. Why? Because microleaks imply large velocity gradients (changes in velocities) near the leaks. But again Weber shear thickening will restrain the leakage rates.

Viscoelasticity and Hysteresis

Consider the sudden emergency closures of shutoff valves in a long transmission line. Navier-Stokes assumes that stress responds instantaneously to strain rate. However Weber-Navier predicts transient strains. Indeed stress lags behind the strain, and therefore Navier-Weber predicts pressure surges which lag behind the strain. The idea is that “water hammer” in Navier-Weber will have delayed pressure surges.

There is actually a key prediction (the \(3 \omega\) harmonic) made by Navier-Weber which is distinct from Navier-Stokes. This is consequence of Large-Amplitude Oscillatory Shear (LAOS).

Imagine the fluid is played between two plates and subjected to rapid sinusoidal shearing motion \(\gamma(t)=\gamma_0 \sin (\omega t)\). When the amplitude \(\gamma_0\) is large at high frequencies \(\omega\), then the fluid is forced beyond the linear regime, and the non-linear shear responses and viscoelasticity are revealed.

In classical Navier-Stokes, the fluids are Newtonian and responds instantaneously to strain rate \(\tau = \mu_0 \dot{\gamma}\). Consequently the pure sine frequency \(\omega\) will have a response output of frequency \(\omega\). However Navier-Weber implies \(\mathbb{T}\) scales cubically with respect to spatial gradient \((\nabla u)^3\). Consequently under high shear, the fluid stress contains a third harmonic \(3\omega\) in the frequency spectrum. I.e. \(\sin^3(\omega t) = \frac{3}{4}\sin(\omega t) - \frac{1}{4}\sin(3\omega t)\). This \(3\omega\) harmonic is a signature of the Navier-Weber model.

Helmholtz Spatial Filter

The term \(Id - \frac{\eta_v}{\rho} \Delta\) is called a spatial Helmholtz Filter in the literature. This factor implies sharp pressure peaks will be rounded off, and sharp pressure peaks are prevented from causing high-frequency oscillations.

Energy Conservation

Fluids are conservative not dissipative. Mechanical work on the fluid will not be immediately converted into thermal heat, but will be stored conservatively in the potential of the fluid. This energy will be elastically returned to the bulk flow.

Ideal Gas Law

To compute these equations of motion in practice requires coupling the pressure \(p\) from \(P\) the sum of all external forces. This is typically obtained from the ideal gas law \(PV=nRT\). The Weber electrodynamic derivation of the ideal gas law is subject of future work.
