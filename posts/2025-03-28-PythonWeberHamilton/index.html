Key ingredients:

  • We construct \(H\) in python as the sum over all \(2\)-body interactions.
  • We compute the numerical gradients of \(H\).
  • We use \(\texttt{minEnergySymmSolve}\) to define effective inertial mass operators \(A\), although we do have alternative formula \(\nabla^2_{vv}H =A\).
  • We review the symplecticity of classical Hamiltonian systems.
  • We define \(\texttt{WeberKick}\) and \(\texttt{WeberDrift}\) updates, and construct \(\texttt{WKDK}\) Leapfrog Verlet integrator.

  • We rescale WH to simulate physical atomic scale.

Computing the Numerical Gradients of \(H\)

The first problem is python coding the Hamiltonian \(H\). Weber is instantaneous action-at-a-distance. A key hypothesis of Weber electrodynamics is that \(N\)-body interactions are determined by the set of all \(2\)-body subinteractions. Hence the total gradients of \(H\) are computed recursively as sums over all \(2\)-body pairs. Thus \[H=\sum'_{ij} H(p_i, p_j)\] is the sum over all pairs of distinct particles \(p_i, p_j\). In section below we construct the numerical Cartesian gradients \(\nabla_{x_i} H(p_i, p_j)\) and \(\nabla_{v_i} H(p_i, p_j)\) using the Newton epsilon limit definition. See the python functions \(\texttt{gradx(H,state)}\) and \(\texttt{gradv(H,state)}\) defined below. We find \(\nabla_{x_i} H= \sum'_j \nabla_{x_i} H(p_i, p_j)\) and \(\nabla_{v_i} H= \sum'_j \nabla_{v_i} H(p_i, p_j).\) We express the gradient using the computational identity \[\texttt{grad(H, state) = [sum([numgrad(H, pi,pj) for pj in state if i,j distinct]) for pi in state]}.\] We tried using \(\texttt{autograd}\) package, but were more successful with explicit numerical approximations.

Weber-Hamilton Equations of Motion

In the above python code we have obtained the space gradient \(\nabla_x H\) and velocity gradient \(\nabla_v H\). The next step is simulating the Weber-Hamilton equations of motion.

Recall the effective mass operator \(A\) which we define as the minimal energy symmetric operator \(A\) satisfying \[x'= v= A^{-1} \nabla_v H.\] The Weber-Hamilton equations of motion is represented by the ODE \[(x', v') = (A^{-1}.\nabla_v H, -A^{-1}. \nabla_x H).\] The hypothesis that \(A\) is symmetric and solves WH equations implies Conservation of Energy is satisfied. Thus we look to numerically compute the operator \[A_{\text{inv}}:=\texttt{minEnergySymmSolve}(\nabla_v H,v).\]

Given the effective mass operator \(A_{\text{inv}}\), the acceleration of the system is defined by \[a=v'=-A_{\text{inv}}. \nabla_x H.\] Thus we compute the accelerations in the given Cartesian coordinates. This makes the WH equations explicit.

What are our options for solving WH equations of motion?

The first principle is that our integrators must satisfy Conservation of Energy. For classical Hamiltonian equations, energy conservation is preserved by the symplectic KDK (“Kick-Drift-Kick”) integrators. But these symplectic integrators are not immediately applicable to the WH equations. In section below we introduce the WKDK integrators (Weber Kick-Drift-Kick).

Remark. We observe that \(\nabla_v H=0\) only if \(v=0\). Therefore \(A_{\text{inv}}\) is well defined even when \(\nabla_v H=0\), with the zero operator being the unique min energy solution mapping \(A.0=0\).

Remark on Classical Symplectic Flows

We are given an ODE \(x'=\phi(t,x)\). Concretely we study \(x' = A^{-1} J \nabla H\). The forward time evolution \(\Phi=\Phi_t\) map gives the time \(t\) solution of the IVP \(x'=\phi(t,x)\), \(x'(0)=t_0\) at time \(t\). The evolution map \(\Phi\) has Jacobian defined by \[J=D_x \Phi.\] Differentiating the ODE system we find \(J\) satisfies \[\frac{d}{dt} J = D_x \phi.\] This is a linear system of ODEs with solution \[J=J(t) = \text{exp}(\int_{t_0}^t D \phi d\tau) .\] Here \(\text{exp}\) denotes matrix exponential.

Product rule implies \(D\phi = D (A J. \nabla H) = (DA)J \nabla H + AJ. D^2 H\).

We note that \(DA\) consists of the tensor object \((\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i^j}A )_{ij}\). This tensor is multiplied by \(J \nabla H\) with equal indices \(ij\) and we obtain a square matrix \(DA. J \nabla H\).

Claim: The Hamiltonian \(H\) is quadratic in the velocity terms. Therefore we expect \(D^2 H\) has constant velocity term, which only depends on relative positions.

Claim: The effective mass operator \(A\) depends only on position, not velocity. I.e. \(D_v A =0\).

Claim: To prove the classical Hamiltonian is symplectic requires proving that \(D\phi\) is a matrix of the form \[D\phi = \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & -{}^t A \end{pmatrix}\] where \(B={}^tB\) and \(C={}^tC\) are symmetric. This is obvious from the classical formula $D = J D^2 H, $ where \[JD^2 H = \begin{pmatrix} \nabla^2_{xv} H & \nabla^2_{vv} H \\ -\nabla^2_{xx} H & -\nabla^2_{vx} H \end{pmatrix}.\] This condition is equivalent to the operator \(D \phi\) being Hamiltonian in the sense of Lie algebras.

We recall the identities \[\nabla_v H = A v\] and \[\nabla^2_{xv} H = \text{grad}_x A . v.\]

Claim: We find \(DA. J \nabla H = D_x A . A v\) and we argue that \(D_v A = 0\). This implies \(\nabla^2_{xv}H=\text{grad}_x A.v\).

Claim: We have \(\nabla^2_{vv} H = \text{grad}_{v} (Av) = A\). This implies an alternative explicit formula for \(A\).

So we obtain the formula \[D \phi = D_x A . Av + A J \nabla^2 H.\] In the classical case \(A\) is diagonal matrix which is independant of \(x\), hence \(D\phi\) obviously satisfies the Hamiltonian Lie algebra conditions and integrates to a symplectic operator \(\Phi=\exp(\int D\phi)\) on state space.

Problem: Can we decide whether \(D \phi\) is a Hamiltonian matrix (skew symmetric) with respect to some almost-complex structure \(\tilde{j}\) on state space?

\(\texttt{WKDK}\) Integrators of WH Equations

We want to numerically integrate the equations of motion such that Conservation of Energy is satisfied. The usual \(\texttt{solve\_ivp}\) method is not sufficient. The classical Hamiltonian, with velocity-independant potential energy and position-independant kinetic energy, has a well-known symplectic integration scheme called Kick-Drift-Kick, c.f. “velocity Verlet” and “leapfrog Verlet” integrators. The basic idea of KDK is building the total Hamiltonian \(H=T+U\) as a combination of exact solutions of \(H_U = U\) and \(H_T=T\). Superposition of these exact solutions defines the KDK integration, and classically is a symplectic transformation on classical state space. In our WH equations of motion, there is no obvious symplectic structure, neither do the Weber Kick and Weber Drift steps admit exact solutions. We define \(\texttt{WeberKick}\) and \(\texttt{WeberDrift}\) updates corresponding to \(H_U\), \(H_T\) respectively.

\(\texttt{WeberDrift}\)

Formally consider the restricted Hamiltonian \(H_T = T = \frac{1}{2} \mu {\dot{r}}^2\). If we were studying particle states strictly with respect to Hamiltonian \(H_T\), then our method would approach the Hamilton equations by first considering the equation $ x’ = v = A_{inv} _v T $ as implicitly defining the inverse effective inertial mass operator \(A_{inv}\) relative to \(H_T\). However for the purposes of numerical integration and Conservation of Energy, we rather define \(A_{inv}\) to be the effective inertial mass operator relative to Weber’s total Hamiltonian \(H\). Therefore we define \[A_{inv} = \texttt{minEnergySymmSolve}(\nabla_v H, v) = (\nabla^2_{vv} H)^{-1} .\]

The second equation is \[v' = a = -A_{inv} \nabla_x T=-(\nabla^2_{vv} H)^{-1}\nabla_x T\] which defines the acceleration of the system. Classically this second equation is zero \(v'=x''=a=0\).

Weber Drift consists of updating \[x^* = x + \Delta t. A_{inv}. \nabla_vT \] and \[ v^* = v - \Delta t. A_{inv}. \nabla_x T .\] These \(x^*, v^*\) are only approximate solutions of the Hamiltonian \(H_T\). The choice of \(A_{inv}\) implies \(T\) is not strictly conserved. However in the composition WKDK this choice will improve stability.

Remark. The classical drift step is the exact solution for systems with only kinetic energy, solving the equation of motion \(\dot{q} = p/m\) in the classical notation. The solution describes uniform motion under constant momentum, which is a classical result of Newtonian mechanics, the so-called Principle of Inertia. Therefore in the absence of external force \(a=v'=0\), a particle moves uniformly with constant momentum. We remark that “inertial mass” is not defined by this Principle of Inertia.

\(\texttt{WeberKick}\)

The Weber Kick update arises from the Hamiltonian \(H_U = U\). Again we choose to define \(A_{inv}\) as the inverse effective inertial mass operator defined relative to the total Weber Hamiltonian \(H\).

We find \[v'=a=- A_{inv} \nabla_x U.\] The Weber Kick update consists of the approximate solution \[x^* = x + \Delta t.A_{inv}\nabla_v U\] and \[v^* = v - \Delta t. A_{inv} . \nabla_x U.\]

Remark. What choice of effective inertial mass should we choose? We think we need keep \(A_{inv}\) equal to the effective inertial mass of the original total system, namely \[A_{inv}:=\texttt{minEnergySymmSolve}(\nabla_v H, v)= \quad \quad \ldots \quad \quad = (\nabla^2_{vv} H)^{-1}.\] In the classical KDK integrators, \(A_{inv}\) is represented by the diagonal inverse mass operator \((\mu_i^{-1} Id_i)_i\) and is constant during the KDK updates.
