Plato’s Cave, Ontological Gaps, and Optimal Compute Frontier

Plato’s Allegory of the Cave is an illustration of the ontological gap (i.e. Efficient Compute Frontier) between shadows and the world.
Author

JHM

Published

November 18, 2025

This article discusses the Compute Optimal Frontier, also known as Efficient Compute Frontier (ECF), or the line that Labs (n.d.). The question is: How does loss (accuracy) asymptotically grow with respect to compute size, parameters, and training data? If we increase the energy budget by 10 percent, then what percentage improvement in error loss can we expect? As ML problems grow larger in scale, there remains the problem of simultaneously optimizing the sizes and scales of training data, number of parameters, and compute power. Empirical studies (Kaplan et al. 2020) have suggested power laws, but these studies admittedy acknowledge a lack of proper theoretical foundation. We propose the line, the so-called ECF, is a measure of the ontological gap between a numerical model and the real world correlations we are trying to predict. Moreover we argue this measure is quantitatively represented by the Tao-Szemeredi Regularization Lemma (Tao 2005), see min program below. Our interpretation highlights the connection between Tao-Szemeredi and optimal solutions of deterministic information bottleneck problems (Strouse and Schwab 2017). Indeed the minimization program can be similarly solved via an agglomerative clustering argument. Thus we look for reductions \(X \mapsto Z\) such that points \(x\) with conditional distributions \(y\mapsto p(y|x)\) on \(Y\) which are “Wasserstein close” to each other are mapped to the same cluster \(x\mapsto z \in Z\). Therefore the optimal \(Z\) is a type of Voronoi partition of the space of conditional distributions \(p(-|x)\) on \(Y\).

We propose the ontological gap between cave dwellers and the world can be numerically formalized by the entropy interpretation of Tao-Szemeredi’s Regularity Lemma (Tao 2005), which introduces an interesting minimization program. Given a parameter space \(X=(X_1, X_2)\) the program looks for coarse subbits \(Z=(Z_1, Z_2)\) which are maximally correlated to the target \(\pi\). Tao-Szemeredi implies that correlations decompose into a deterministic part and a random statistically independant part. The representation contains only so many significant coarse bits. When we condition on these most significant bits, everything else is random.

Our explanation of ECF implies there are no homogeneous asymptotic scaling laws for the error loss of ML models. This implies the ECF does not asymptotically approach zero. Instead we predict the ECF asymptotically limits to the entropy uncertainty of the given representation. In the literature this is sometimes known as the Bayes floor and formalized by Fano’s Inequality.

The Tao-Szemeredi program (Tao 2005) looks to construct the best coarse approximation to predicting a given correlation. The idea is analogous to diophantine approximation, i.e. find the best approximation with the simplest coefficients. These coarse approximations are the critical insights basic to advertising, e.g. [https://www.criticalmass.com]{Critical Mass}, and provide coarse bits are the most statistically significant, and such that \(\pi\) is independant of conditioning on any further bits of \(Z\).

The point of TS is to generate reductions \(Z, Z'\) which have bounded entropy without assuming a priori any bounds on the entropy of \(\pi\) or \(X_1, X_2\). Thus we claim Tao-Szemeredi balances optimization and generalization, and argue that the construction of the best approximations \(Z, Z'\) ought to be the universal goal of machine learning.

Now we introduce the minimization program which defines these significant bits. In fact Tao-Szemeredi provides two approximations, the coarse and fine approximations \(Z, Z'\). Both \(Z,Z'\) have bounded entropy, independant of \(H(\pi)\). Both \(Z,Z'\) are coarse approximations, but \(Z'\) is finer satisfying \(X \mapsto Z' \mapsto Z\) and having a certain optimality property with respect to deterministic reductions \(W\) satisfying the determinism constraints \(X \mapsto W\).

The goal of the minimization program is to simultaneously minimize the conditional entropy \(H(\pi|Z')\) and the relative entropy \(H(Z')/H(F(Z))\). Let \(\epsilon > 0\) be choice of small real number. Let \(F:{\mathbf{R}}_+ \to {\mathbf{R}}_+\) be a non-decreasing function. The technical assumptions of TS is that \(\pi\) has finite mean, and that \(X_1, X_2\) are random variables. We initialize with \(Z_1=0\) and \(Z_2=0\).

Let \(Z'=(Z'_1, Z'_2)\) be a minimizer of \[\begin{equation} \text{min}_{Z'}[H(\pi|Z')+\frac{H(Z')}{F(H(Z))}] \label{eq:tsmin} \end{equation}\] where the minimum ranges over all deterministic reductions \(Z'\) satisfying \(X\mapsto Z' \mapsto Z\).

If \(H(\pi|Z)-H(\pi|Z') \geq \epsilon\), then replace \(Z:=Z'\) and restart the minimization program. Otherwise break, and return \(Z, Z'\) the coarse and fine approximations, respectively.
